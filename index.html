CHAPTER 01  •  QUARTER 1: BUILDING AI LITERACY FOR HEALTHCARE

Why Every Doctor Needs AI Literacy

Understand it. Question it. Lead it.

1 Opening perspective

AI is already part of healthcare. It helps write notes, reads some scans and answers questions that patients type into chatbots. In a March 2026 poll by KFF, about one in three US adults said they had used an AI chatbot for health information in the past year. Doctors who understand AI can guide patients and protect them. Doctors who do not may find the tools guiding them instead.

2 The science behind the topic

AI literacy means knowing enough about AI to use it safely, question it and explain it to others. Researchers describe it as the skills to judge AI critically, to work with it and to use it as a tool. You do not need to write code. Think of reading an ECG: you do not build the machine, but you must know when not to trust it.

  • Artificial intelligence (AI): computer programs that do tasks that normally need human thinking
  • Machine learning: AI that finds patterns in data instead of following only fixed rules
  • Deep learning: machine learning with many layers, often used for images
  • Generative AI: AI that creates new text, images or audio, such as chatbots

3 Clinical relevance

The World Health Organization’s 2024 guidance on large multimodal models names five areas of use in health: clinical care, patients using AI themselves, paperwork and notes, education and research. It also warns about false statements, bias, over-trust in the tool and security risks.

In a 2023 study of ChatGPT-written literature reviews, more than half of the references from GPT-3.5 and about one in five from GPT-4 were made up. AI can sound sure while being wrong.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Treat AI output as a draft from a junior colleague.
  2. Check facts, doses and references against a trusted source.
  3. Never put names, phone numbers or ID numbers into public AI tools.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain bronchiolitis to the parents of an infant at a Grade 6 reading level. Include warning signs that need urgent medical attention. Use short sentences and bullet points.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Made-up facts: AI can invent details and references.
  • Privacy: public tools are not built for identifiable patient data.
  • Bias: tools can work less well for some groups of patients.
  • Over-trust: a fluent answer is not the same as a correct one.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI is already in healthcare. Staying out does not keep us safe.
  2. AI literacy is a core skill alongside reading an ECG or a lab report.
  3. The doctor stays accountable. AI assists. The doctor decides.

7 Discussion question

Where have you already used AI in your clinical, teaching or research work, and what is one thing it got wrong? Share your thoughts in the comments.

CHAPTER 02  •  QUARTER 1: BUILDING AI LITERACY FOR HEALTHCARE

Artificial Intelligence Explained for Doctors

Know the family before you trust the tool.

1 Opening perspective

In one week you may hear “AI” used for a smart ECG, a note-writing tool, a scheduling system and a chatbot. They are very different tools with different risks. Learning the main types is like learning drug classes: you cannot judge a tool until you know which family it belongs to.

2 The science behind the topic

The term artificial intelligence was first used in a 1955 proposal by John McCarthy and colleagues for a workshop at Dartmouth in 1956. Today it covers several approaches. Older systems follow rules written by people. Machine learning learns patterns from data.

Picture three circles. AI is the largest circle. Machine learning sits inside it. Deep learning sits inside machine learning. Generative AI is a branch that creates new content.

  • Machine learning: learns patterns from data, for example flagging unusual results
  • Deep learning: finds patterns in complex data such as images
  • Generative AI: creates new text, images or audio
  • Large language model (LLM): a generative AI trained on large amounts of text to predict likely words

3 Clinical relevance

In 2018 the US FDA authorized IDx-DR, the first AI system allowed to give a diabetic eye screening result without a specialist reading the image. It is authorized only for adults aged 22 and over. A tool tested in adults is not automatically safe for children.

The FDA’s public list of AI-enabled medical devices now holds well over a thousand entries, and most are radiology tools. A 2025 study in JAMA Health Forum found that devices without clinical validation data were more likely to be recalled. Regulatory clearance is not proof that a tool helps patients.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what task the tool was built for.
  2. Ask who it was tested on, including children.
  3. Ask what happens when it is wrong and who is responsible.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain the difference between machine learning and generative AI to a medical student. Use one pediatric example for each and keep it under 150 words.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Label confusion: not everything called “AI” works the same way.
  • Population mismatch: a tool tested in adults may fail in children.
  • Clearance is not proof of benefit.
  • Fluent errors: generative AI can sound right and be wrong.

6 Key takeaways

  1. “AI” is a family, not one thing.
  2. Task and data define a tool. Ask what it was built for and who it was tested on.
  3. Check children separately. Adult results do not guarantee pediatric safety.

CHAPTER 03  •  QUARTER 1: BUILDING AI LITERACY FOR HEALTHCARE

How Does AI Actually Learn?

AI learns from examples. You judge the results.

1 Opening perspective

You learned to read a chest X-ray by seeing many films, each with a known answer, and by being corrected. Many AI tools learn in a similar way. The difference is that they only know the examples they were shown. They have never done a ward round or met the patient behind the image.

2 The science behind the topic

Every AI learning system needs three things: data (the examples), an algorithm (the method for learning from them) and feedback (a way to measure and correct mistakes).

Inside a neural network, learning is a loop. The network makes a guess, compares it with the right answer, adjusts its internal settings (called weights) to reduce the error, and repeats this many times.

To check that it truly learned, the data is split. A training set is used to learn, a validation set to tune, and a test set is kept back as an honest exam on cases it has never seen.

  • Supervised learning: learns from examples with known answers, such as X-rays labelled “pneumonia” or “no pneumonia”
  • Unsupervised learning: looks for groups in data that has no labels
  • Reinforcement learning: learns by trial and error with rewards
  • Overfitting: the model memorised its examples and does poorly on new cases

3 Clinical relevance

A 2018 study in PLOS Medicine trained deep learning models to find pneumonia on chest X-rays from three hospital systems. In most comparisons the models did better on data from their own hospital than on data from other hospitals, and they could even tell which hospital an image came from. A tool can look excellent where it was trained and disappoint in yours.

Children are a special gap. A review of FDA-authorized AI devices published in JAMA Pediatrics found that only about one in six was labelled for use in children. One analysis of public medical imaging datasets found that children made up under 2% of patients.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what data the tool learned from.
  2. Ask whether it was tested on data from another hospital.
  3. Ask whether children were in the training and testing data.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain overfitting to a medical student in 100 words, using an analogy from pediatric training. Then give me one question to ask an AI vendor about it.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Shortcut learning: a model may use clues such as the hospital of origin instead of the disease.
  • Optimistic results: tests on the developer’s own data can overstate real performance.
  • Adult-first data: children are under-represented in many datasets.
  • Black box: it can be hard to see why a model gave an answer.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI learns from examples, not experience. It only knows what its data contained.
  2. Learning is a loop: guess, measure the error, adjust, repeat.
  3. Test on new data. Ask where, and on whom, the tool was tested, including children.

CHAPTER 04  •  QUARTER 1: BUILDING AI LITERACY FOR HEALTHCARE

Generative AI: Beyond ChatGPT

A powerful drafting assistant, not a clinician.

1 Opening perspective

For many doctors, “generative AI” means one chatbot. In reality ChatGPT is one product among many, and the same technology is appearing in note-taking tools, patient portals and imaging software. This chapter looks past the brand name at what generative AI is, what it can do and where the evidence says to be careful.

2 The science behind the topic

Generative AI creates new content such as text, images or audio. A large language model (LLM) is trained on huge amounts of text to predict likely next words, which is why it writes fluent answers. ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are LLM chatbots.

When an LLM can also take in other kinds of input, such as images, it is called a large multimodal model. WHO describes these as models that accept one or more types of input, such as text, images and video, and give outputs that are not limited to the type of input.

  • Chatbot: an LLM you talk to in a chat window
  • Ambient scribe: a tool that listens to a consultation and drafts a note for the clinician to review
  • Multimodal model: an AI that works with more than one type of data

3 Clinical relevance

Real users matter. In a 2026 Nature Medicine trial of about 1,300 UK adults, language models given medical scenarios on their own named the right condition in about 95% of cases. When members of the public used the same models, they named it in fewer than 35% of cases, no better than people who used their usual methods.

Pediatric diagnosis. In a 2024 JAMA Pediatrics study, an earlier version of ChatGPT (GPT-3.5) gave the correct diagnosis in only 17 of 100 complex pediatric case challenges. Newer models are better, but fluent writing is not the same as diagnosing.

Ambient scribes are the clearest early benefit. In a 2025 study, clinician-reported burnout fell from about half to about four in ten after 30 days of use. Results are uneven, and in one randomized trial only one of two scribes reduced note-writing time.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Give context, not identity: describe the task and remove names and ID numbers.
  2. Ask for sources, then open each one. References can be invented.
  3. Check doses, diagnoses and facts against a trusted guideline before using them.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft a one-page, Grade 6 reading-level handout for parents about caring for a child with a fever at home. Include when to seek urgent care. Use short headings and bullet points. Mark any statement I should verify against a guideline.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Invented facts and references.
  • Benchmark-to-bedside gap: good scores alone do not predict good results with real users.
  • Over-trust and bias: WHO warns that errors may be overlooked.
  • Privacy: do not paste identifiable data into public tools.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Generative AI is more than one chatbot.
  2. Real-world evidence beats demos.
  3. Draft with AI, decide as a doctor. Verify every fact before it reaches a patient.

Understanding Large Language Models

Know how it works to know where it breaks.

1 Opening perspective

A large language model can write a polished discharge summary in seconds and, in the next breath, invent a reference that does not exist. Both come from the same design. Three ideas explain most of what you will see: tokens, the context window and next-word prediction.

2 The science behind the topic

An LLM is trained on huge amounts of text to predict what comes next. On its own it does not look facts up in a database. It builds an answer one small piece at a time, choosing likely continuations from patterns it has learned. That is why the answer sounds fluent whether or not it is true.

  • Token: a small piece of text, often part of a word. Models read and write in tokens
  • Context window: the most text, counted in tokens, that a model can consider at once, including your question and its reply
  • Next-word prediction: choosing a likely next token, again and again

3 Clinical relevance

Position matters. A 2024 study found that language models did best when key information was at the start or end of a long input, and worse when it was in the middle.

People-pleasing. A 2025 study in npj Digital Medicine asked five LLMs to write messages that treated a brand-name drug and its generic as different medicines. The GPT models complied every time, even though they could match the drug names correctly. When they were told they could refuse illogical requests and were asked to recall the facts first, most refused. A wrong idea in your question can become a wrong answer.

Language matters. A 2023 study found that the same text can need many times more tokens in some languages than in English, which uses up the context window faster.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Put your question and the key facts at the start or end of long text.
  2. Invite pushback: tell the model it may say a request is wrong.
  3. Ask it to state what it knows first, then do the task.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Before answering, list what you know about the medicine involved. If anything in my request is incorrect or illogical, say so instead of complying. Then draft a short safety note for parents about giving paracetamol to a child, and flag anything I should check against the product label.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Plausible but wrong answers.
  • Agreeing too easily with a flawed question.
  • Missing details in long inputs.
  • Models change quickly, so test any tool on your own tasks.

6 Key takeaways

  1. LLMs predict text. They do not look facts up.
  2. The context window is a limit, and position matters.
  3. LLMs tend to agree with you. State premises carefully.

Prompt Engineering for Doctors

Clear prompts help. Checking still comes first.

1 Opening perspective

Prompt engineering sounds technical, but it is really clear communication, the same skill you use when you write a good referral letter or brief a junior colleague. A prompt is the instruction you give an AI. A 2023 tutorial in the Journal of Medical Internet Research described prompt engineering as designing and refining prompts to guide an AI’s output, and called it an emerging skill for medical professionals.

2 The science behind the topic

A strong prompt gives the AI what a trainee would need.

  • Task: exactly what you want done, such as summarize, compare, draft or explain
  • Context: the setting and the facts that matter, with identifiers removed
  • Audience: who will read the result, such as parents or students
  • Format: length, structure, reading level and tone
  • Example (optional): a sample of the style you want

3 Clinical relevance

A 2024 scoping review of 114 studies on prompt engineering in medicine found that techniques such as giving examples or asking the model to reason step by step were common, but that about six in ten of the prompt-design studies did not compare against a baseline without them. The evidence on which tricks work is still thin.

The same review noted that several studies had sent sensitive clinical data to ChatGPT, which is a privacy risk.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Write the task in one clear sentence.
  2. Add context without patient identifiers, then name the audience and format.
  3. Read the draft and refine it: “shorter”, “simpler”, “add warning signs”.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Create five single-best-answer questions for final-year medical students on childhood asthma control. Give four options each, mark the correct answer and add a one-line explanation. Use simple language and tell me which questions I should check against current guidelines.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • No magic words: a trick that helps one tool may not help another.
  • A good prompt cannot guarantee true facts or real references.
  • Privacy: remove identifiers first.
  • Over-trusting your own prompt: always review the output.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Prompting is clear communication: task, context, audience and format.
  2. Iterate: read the first draft, then refine.
  3. No prompt replaces checking.

CHAPTER 07  •  QUARTER 1: BUILDING AI LITERACY FOR HEALTHCARE

ChatGPT vs Gemini vs Claude

Choose the tool for the task, not the brand.

1 Opening perspective

Which AI assistant is best for doctors? ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are the three most talked-about general-purpose assistants. The honest answer from the evidence is that the best choice depends on the task, the version and the setting. This chapter gives you a fair way to compare them instead of picking a winner.

2 The science behind the topic

All three are large language models with a chat app (Chapter 5). They differ in the company behind them, the model version, extra features and the privacy terms of each plan. These details change every few months, so compare on three things.

  • Model version: results belong to one version, and versions change often
  • Task fit: a tool can lead on exam questions and trail on patient communication
  • Plan and privacy: free consumer plans are not designed for identifiable patient data

3 Clinical relevance

In a June 2026 study in Nature Medicine from NYU Langone, three leading models formed a top tier with no significant differences between them. All three scored higher than two specialized clinical tools on exam-style questions and on real physician queries.

Soon afterwards, an early report that had not yet been peer reviewed reached a different conclusion about one of the specialized tools. It used different questions, different graders and different model versions, and that tool’s developer helped run the study. Studies can disagree for many reasons, and no single result settles which tool is best.

Privacy is part of the comparison. Free consumer versions of these chatbots are not designed for identifiable patient data. Companies offer business or healthcare plans that may include a signed data agreement, but the terms differ and change, so check each vendor’s current terms.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Pick three real tasks that contain no patient identifiers.
  2. Give every tool the identical prompt.
  3. Score the answers blind for accuracy, clarity and safety, and verify facts and references.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain asthma inhaler technique to the parents of a 6-year-old in simple language. Use short steps and include warning signs that need urgent care. Keep it under 200 words.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Results age fast: studies describe versions that are often replaced within months.
  • Conflicts of interest: check who designed and paid for a study.
  • Exam scores are not clinical performance.
  • Patient data: never paste identifiable data into a consumer plan.

6 Key takeaways

  1. There is no permanent winner. Results depend on task, version, test and date.
  2. Read the study design: who asked the questions, who graded, who paid?
  3. Test on your own tasks and protect patient data.

From Search Engines to AI Research Assistants

AI finds fast. Experts confirm.

1 Opening perspective

For two decades, finding medical information meant typing keywords into a search engine or PubMed and reading the results yourself. Today AI tools answer in full sentences, cite papers and even screen studies. That saves time, but the answer now arrives pre-digested and the sources are easy to skip.

2 The science behind the topic

Database search (such as PubMed) matches your keywords and you judge the results yourself. AI search summaries add an AI-written answer above web results. AI research assistants such as Elicit, Consensus and Scite search papers and summarize or extract findings.

Many of these tools combine search with a language model so that answers are built from retrieved documents. Chapter 9 explains how that works.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2025 study in Cochrane Evidence Synthesis and Methods compared Elicit with the original searches of four systematic reviews. Elicit found fewer than half of the included studies, against more than nine in ten for the traditional searches, although more of what it found was relevant. The authors judged it useful as a supplement, not a replacement.

A 2026 comparison of AI literature tools in cardiology found large differences between tools and supported combining human and AI searching. The authors called their findings preliminary.

In January 2026 a Guardian investigation reported that Google AI Overviews gave misleading answers on some health topics, and Google later removed some health summaries. Google said most overviews are accurate and link to reputable sources.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Write a clear question: who, what treatment, compared with what, which outcome.
  2. Ask for sources, then open each one to check it exists and says what the summary claims.
  3. Run your own PubMed search as well and compare.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Find recent randomized trials or systematic reviews on inhaled corticosteroids for preschool wheeze. For each, give the citation, study type, population and main finding. Say clearly if you cannot find a source.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Missed studies: AI tools can miss many relevant papers.
  • Invented or misread references: check every citation.
  • Top-of-page authority: an AI summary looks official even when wrong.
  • Big differences between tools, topics and versions.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI search is fast, not complete.
  2. Open the paper before you trust the summary.
  3. Test the tool on a question whose answer you already know.

Understanding RAG in Healthcare

Retrieval gives AI a reference. You still check the reference.

1 Opening perspective

Picture two colleagues asked the same question. One answers from memory. The other has the guideline open on the desk and can point to the page. Retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, gives an AI assistant that open book.

2 The science behind the topic

A RAG system works in three steps. It retrieves relevant passages from a chosen collection such as guidelines, papers or local protocols. It adds them to the question. Then it writes an answer based on them. This lets a tool use current guidelines or local protocols without retraining, and it is meant to reduce made-up answers and show the sources.

The idea is simple. The answer is only as good as the documents it finds and how faithfully the model uses them.

  • Plain LLM: answers from patterns learned in training, usually without sources
  • RAG system: looks up documents first, then answers from them

3 Clinical relevance

A 2025 review and meta-analysis of 20 studies in JAMIA found that RAG-based systems did better on average than the same language models without retrieval on clinical tasks.

But a citation is not proof. In a 2025 Nature Communications study, seven language models answered 800 medical questions. Between half and nine in ten of the responses were not fully supported by the sources they cited, and some sources contradicted the statements.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what documents the tool searches: which guidelines, years and countries.
  2. Open the citation and find the sentence that supports the claim.
  3. Ask for the supporting quote for each statement and check it yourself.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Using only the guideline text pasted below, answer my question. Quote the sentence that supports each statement. If the text does not contain the answer, say so.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Unsupported statements even when the link is real.
  • Missing documents: if retrieval misses the key guideline, so does the answer.
  • Out-of-date or non-local sources.
  • False reassurance: a citation looks like proof, so open it.

6 Key takeaways

  1. RAG is open-book AI.
  2. It helps on average, but a citation is not proof.
  3. Check the library the tool searches.

AI Agents: The Next Evolution

Agents act. Keep a human in the loop.

1 Opening perspective

A chatbot answers. An AI agent acts. Instead of only drafting text, an agent can plan steps, use tools such as a calendar or a health record, and carry out a task with little supervision. The promise is less paperwork. The risk is that mistakes now come with actions attached.

2 The science behind the topic

An AI agent is usually a language model inside a loop with four parts: a goal, a plan, tools it can use and memory of what has happened so far. With a chatbot, you carry out any action yourself. With an agent, errors can reach real systems, so checks must come earlier.

3 Clinical relevance

In MedAgentBench, published in NEJM AI in 2025, agents were tested on 300 clinician-written tasks in a simulated electronic health record. The best model succeeded on about 70% of tasks, results varied widely by task type, and the authors concluded that agents are not yet reliable enough to work unsupervised.

A December 2025 study in JAMA Network Open tested prompt injection, meaning hidden instructions placed in the text an AI reads. It led to unsafe medical recommendations in more than nine in ten simulated cases, including most of the highest-risk ones. An agent that reads outside text could face the same trick.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what the agent can read and what it can change.
  2. Require a person to approve each important action.
  3. Start with drafting tasks, not sending or ordering.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft, but do not send, a friendly reminder message to parents about their child’s next vaccination visit. Show me the message and list any information you used. Wait for my approval before taking any action.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Errors with consequences: a wrong step can change real records or messages.
  • Limited reliability in current tests.
  • Manipulation by hidden instructions.
  • Too much access: give only the permissions a task needs.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Agents act, chatbots answer. That raises benefit and risk.
  2. Treat agents as supervised assistants.
  3. Design guardrails first: limit access, keep logs, require sign-off.

Understanding AI Hallucinations

Fluent is not the same as factual.

1 Opening perspective

In AI, a hallucination is an answer that sounds confident but is false or unsupported: an invented lab value, a made-up syndrome or a reference that does not exist. Earlier chapters mentioned hallucinations several times. This chapter explains why they happen and why they matter.

2 The science behind the topic

An LLM predicts likely text. Likely is not the same as true. When a fact is missing from its training or from your question, the model can fill the gap with something plausible. Researchers note that hallucination may be built into how all LLMs work, so the aim is to manage it, not expect it to vanish.

  • Fabricated detail: an invented fact, value or condition
  • Fabricated reference: a citation to a paper that is not real or does not support the claim
  • Elaboration: repeating and expanding a false detail you gave it
  • Omission: leaving out something important

3 Clinical relevance

In a 2025 study in Communications Medicine, six language models were given 300 doctor-written cases, each with one made-up detail such as a fake lab test or sign. The models repeated or expanded on the fake detail in roughly half to four in five cases. Adding a short caution to the prompt reduced the average rate from about two-thirds to under half, but did not remove the problem.

A 2025 study in npj Digital Medicine reviewed about 13,000 sentences from 450 AI-written clinical notes. About 1 to 2 sentences in 100 were hallucinations, and 44% of those could have affected diagnosis or management if uncorrected. Omissions were more common than hallucinations.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Add a caution to your prompt: use only validated information and flag anything you cannot verify.
  2. Do not feed the AI terms you are unsure exist.
  3. Verify numbers, doses, names and references.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Use only clinically validated information. If any term, value or claim in my text is unfamiliar, unverifiable or possibly incorrect, flag it instead of explaining it. Then summarise the text below in five bullet points.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Confident tone hides errors.
  • Prompts reduce but do not remove the problem.
  • Omissions are silent: what is missing is harder to notice than what is wrong.
  • Rates change between models and tasks.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Hallucination is built into how LLMs work. Plan for it.
  2. Verification is the real safeguard.
  3. Look for what is missing as well as what is wrong.

Multimodal AI in Healthcare

More inputs, same duty to verify.

1 Opening perspective

Medicine is multimodal. We combine the history, the examination, the images, the labs and what the family tells us. Multimodal AI tries to do something similar with models that take in text, images, audio and other data together.

2 The science behind the topic

As Chapter 4 explained, WHO describes large multimodal models as accepting more than one type of input. In practice this covers text (notes and letters), images (a photo or scan), audio (a recorded consultation) and combinations of these. Every extra type of input is another place where errors can start.

3 Clinical relevance

Images. In a 2024 study in npj Digital Medicine using 207 NEJM Image Challenge questions, GPT-4V scored 81.6% against 77.8% for physicians. But in about one in three of its correct answers, the reasoning was flawed, most often in describing the image. It can be right for the wrong reasons.

Audio. In the Careless Whisper study (2024), roughly 1% of transcriptions by OpenAI’s Whisper contained phrases or sentences that nobody spoke, and about four in ten of those invented passages included harmful content. Errors were more likely with long pauses, as in speakers with aphasia. These figures describe versions tested in 2023.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask the AI to describe what it sees or hears before it interprets.
  2. Check the description yourself against the image or recording.
  3. Treat transcripts as drafts and listen again to the key parts.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Describe only what you can see in this image. Then list possible interpretations as hypotheses, not conclusions, and say what cannot be determined from the image alone.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Right answer, wrong reasoning.
  • Invented speech in transcripts.
  • Uneven performance: some speakers and patients may be served worse.
  • Identifiable data: faces and voices can identify a person.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Multimodal AI combines inputs, and every input adds a way to go wrong.
  2. Check the reasoning, not just the answer.
  3. Test on your own patient population before you trust a tool.

Evaluating AI Tools Before Using Them

Trust is earned with evidence from your own setting.

1 Opening perspective

Every AI tool arrives with confident claims. Before it touches patients or notes you need a simple way to ask: does it work, for whom, and at what risk? You already do this for drugs and devices. AI deserves the same habit.

2 The science behind the topic

The FUTURE-AI guideline, published in the BMJ in 2025 by 117 experts from 50 countries, sets out six principles for trustworthy healthcare AI. In plain words, ask six questions: Is it fair to all patient groups? Does it work outside the place it was built? Can it be checked and monitored? Is it usable in our workflow? Does it stay reliable and secure? Can we understand why it gave an answer?

  • Fairness: works across patient groups
  • Universality: works outside the development site
  • Traceability: can be audited and monitored
  • Usability: fits real workflows
  • Robustness: stays reliable and secure
  • Explainability: gives reasons we can understand

3 Clinical relevance

A widely used sepsis warning tool was tested independently at one US hospital system on about 38,000 hospital stays (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021). It performed worse than the developer had reported, missed about two in three patients with sepsis and raised an alert for nearly one in five of all patients. The developer disputed some of the conclusions, but the main message stands: tools need independent, local testing.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Purpose: what exact task, and who is accountable?
  2. Evidence: was it tested outside the developer’s site, and were children included?
  3. Monitoring: who checks performance after it goes live?

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“I am evaluating an AI documentation tool for a pediatric outpatient clinic. Write ten questions to ask the vendor about validation, children, privacy, errors and ongoing monitoring. Do not assume any vendor claim is true.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Brochure versus bedside: vendor-reported performance may not match real life.
  • Local differences in patients and workflows.
  • Alert fatigue from too many false alarms.
  • One-off testing: performance can drift over time.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Evaluate AI like a drug or device.
  2. Insist on local evidence, including children.
  3. Plan monitoring from day one.

AI in Clinical Decision Support

A second opinion, never the final word.

1 Opening perspective

Clinical decision support means tools that help you decide: alerts, risk scores, diagnostic suggestions and treatment prompts. Older tools followed fixed rules. AI-based tools learn patterns from data or use language models to suggest answers. The practical question is simple: do doctors working with AI decide better for real patients?

2 The science behind the topic

There are three common kinds. Rule-based tools follow if-then rules written by experts, such as a drug-interaction alert. Predictive models learn from past data to estimate risk, such as a sepsis warning score. Language-model assistants write reasoning and suggestions in text, such as a chatbot proposing a differential diagnosis.

The key question is not whether AI can answer a written case. It is whether doctors using AI make better decisions than doctors without it.

3 Clinical relevance

Diagnosis. In a 2024 randomized trial in JAMA Network Open, 50 physicians worked through six written cases. Doctors with GPT-4 scored about 76% and doctors with usual resources about 74%, which was not a significant difference. GPT-4 on its own scored about 92%. Having the tool did not make the doctors better at diagnosis.

Management. In a 2025 Nature Medicine trial of 92 physicians working through management cases, GPT-4 assistance improved scores by about 6.5 percentage points over usual resources, although doctors spent a little longer per case. The authors say the result should be confirmed in real clinical practice.

Both trials used written cases, not live patients, and one model (GPT-4). In pediatrics, check whether a tool was tested in children (Chapters 2 and 3).

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Form your own view first, then ask AI what you may have missed.
  2. Ask for reasons for and against each option, and the next test for each.
  3. Check the suggestion against a guideline before you act.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Here is a de-identified case summary: [paste]. List the five most likely diagnoses with the findings for and against each, and the next test for each. Then tell me what key information is missing.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Teams do not add up automatically: doctors with AI did no better at diagnosis in one trial.
  • A written case is not a patient.
  • Automation bias: users may defer to the tool.
  • Accountability stays with the doctor.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Results depend on the task. Management improved while diagnosis did not significantly.
  2. Form your own view first.
  3. Use AI as a second opinion, not a decision-maker.

AI in Pediatric Practice

Children are not small adults, and neither are their AI tools.

1 Opening perspective

A pediatric OPD runs on conversation: the history from parents, reassurance, instructions, follow-up messages and school letters. AI can touch almost every step. But pediatrics has features that general tools often miss: age-dependent norms, weight-based doses, two audiences (child and parent) and confidentiality for adolescents.

2 The science behind the topic

In pediatrics, a tool adapted from adult data is not the same as a tool built and tested for children. Four things to check every time: age and development, who the audience is (parent or child), confidentiality for teenagers, and dose safety.

3 Clinical relevance

In 2026 the American Academy of Pediatrics published a policy statement on generative AI in pediatric care. Its main messages include:

  • Tools should be designed with pediatric data, not just adapted from adult models.
  • Children are under-represented in training data, and few tools have been tested specifically in children.
  • Institutions should check privacy, conflicts of interest and pediatric testing before adopting a tool, because public chatbots often do not meet health privacy rules.
  • Clinicians keep oversight and responsibility, and should be open with families when AI is used.

A 2024 JAMA Pediatrics study looked at 300 messages from adolescents and from parents or other proxies. A language model correctly recognized about three in four of the messages sent by proxies, and in under 1% of cases the AI draft gave confidential information to a caregiver. A draft that ignores who is writing can breach a teenager’s privacy.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. State the age and the audience (parent, teenager, school), never a name.
  2. Ask the AI to flag every dose or age-specific statement for you to verify.
  3. Ask for simple words, short steps and warning signs.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft a one-page handout for parents of a 3-year-old with a mild cold: how to keep the child comfortable, when to seek urgent care and when to call the clinic. Use Grade 6 language and short bullet points. Do not include medicine doses. Flag every statement I should verify against a guideline.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Adult-trained tools may not suit children.
  • Confidentiality slips with adolescents.
  • Privacy settings: keep identifiers out of public tools.
  • Dose and age errors: verify against a formulary.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Children need tools built and tested for children.
  2. Ask who is writing: parent, teenager or clinician.
  3. Keep oversight and be open with families.

AI in Asthma and Allergy Care

Helpful for education, risky for unsupervised advice.

1 Opening perspective

Parents of children with asthma or food allergy ask questions at all hours. Can my child exercise today? Is this reaction anaphylaxis? When do I use the adrenaline auto-injector? Chatbots now answer these questions. Asthma and allergy are good test cases because the safe answer often depends on small details.

2 The science behind the topic

Advice for asthma and allergy depends on the child’s history, severity and triggers. A general answer can be wrong for this child. AI is being used for three jobs here: explaining conditions to parents, answering guideline questions and suggesting diagnoses in case scenarios. Each has its own risks.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2025 study in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice put 120 allergy questions to ChatGPT. Accuracy was reasonable overall, but more than half of the answers contained errors and about 5% had critical errors. One example was recommending hypoallergenic formulas broadly for children with confirmed cow’s milk allergy, which could be dangerous without clarification. Most answers were written at a high reading level. The authors concluded that it is not reliable for unsupervised use.

Smaller studies that asked chatbots asthma questions have generally found good accuracy on average, with errors in questions that depend on context.

These studies describe particular versions at one point in time. Emergency action plans must always come from a clinician, never from a chatbot.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask for a Grade 6 reading level and warning signs in plain words.
  2. Keep action plans clinician-owned.
  3. Ask families what they read, and check triggers, allergens and severity.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Write a one-page, Grade 6 explanation of an asthma action plan for parents of a 7-year-old, describing the green, yellow and red zones in general terms. Do not include medicine names or doses; I will add them from the child’s plan. Flag anything I should verify against current guidelines.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Critical errors exist.
  • Inconsistency: the same question can get different answers.
  • Weak sources: citations may be wrong.
  • Hard-to-read answers.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI can help families learn, but only with your review.
  2. Emergency and action plans are clinician-owned.
  3. Check the details that change the answer: triggers, allergens and severity.

AI in Medical Imaging

AI is a second reader, not a replacement.

1 Opening perspective

Imaging is where AI in medicine is most mature. Images are digital, come with expert reports and exist in huge numbers. That is why most AI devices on the FDA list are radiology tools. The practical question is where AI should sit in the workflow: as a sorter, a second reader, or on its own?

2 The science behind the topic

Imaging AI mostly uses deep learning (Chapter 2) trained on images labelled by experts (Chapter 3). It usually plays one of three roles: triage (sorting cases by risk), second reader (marking findings for the reader) or autonomous screening (giving a result without a clinician reading the image).

3 Clinical relevance

Where it helps. In Sweden’s MASAI trial of about 106,000 women, AI risk scores were used to decide whether a mammogram needed one or two readers. AI-supported screening found about 29% more cancers without more false alarms, cut the reading workload by about 44%, and in the final results led to about 12% fewer cancers appearing between screenings.

Where it can mislead. In a 2023 study in Radiology, 27 radiologists read 50 mammograms with AI-labelled suggestions. When the suggestion was right, accuracy was about 80%. When it was wrong, accuracy fell sharply, to about 20% for less experienced readers and about 45% for the most experienced. Experience reduced this over-trust, but did not remove it.

For children, ask for pediatric testing: children make up very little of public imaging data (Chapter 3), and the first autonomous screening tool was authorized only for adults (Chapter 2).

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what role the tool plays: triage, second reader or standalone.
  2. Form your own impression before you look at the AI output.
  3. Ask for pediatric results and the false-alarm rate.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain to a medical student the difference between AI triage, AI as a second reader and autonomous AI screening in radiology. Give one risk and one safeguard for each.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Automation bias: a wrong suggestion can pull even experts off course.
  • Pediatric data gaps.
  • Results may not transfer to other systems and settings.
  • More detection is not automatically better: look for benefit in outcomes.

6 Key takeaways

  1. The role in the workflow matters.
  2. Beware automation bias: read first, then compare.
  3. Check children separately.

AI in Laboratory Medicine

Numbers need context. So does AI.

1 Opening perspective

A lab report is a column of numbers beside a reference range. In many health systems patients now see results directly in a portal, often before a clinician can explain them, and many paste the numbers into a chatbot. AI can explain results well, and it can also misread them.

2 The science behind the topic

A lab value needs context. Is it abnormal? The reference range depends on age, sex, method and laboratory. What does it mean? That depends on symptoms, history and trend. What next? That depends on the whole clinical picture. An AI sees only the numbers you give it, not the child in front of you. Pediatric reference ranges change with age, so always give the age and the laboratory’s own range.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2024 study in the Journal of Medical Internet Research compared four language models and human forum answers on lab-test questions from lay people. GPT-4 answers were more accurate, helpful and safe than the other models and the human answers. Even so, AI answers could lack the person’s context, contain incorrect statements or give no references.

A 2026 study in the same journal asked language models to turn de-identified lab results into questions a patient could ask. The quality varied, and the authors say human review is needed before such text reaches patients.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Give the laboratory’s own reference range, the units and the child’s age.
  2. Include previous values, because a trend says more than one number.
  3. Ask it to explain, not diagnose, and to say what information is missing.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Here are de-identified lab results with the laboratory’s own reference ranges and the child’s age: [paste]. Explain in plain language what each value measures and which values fall outside the range. Do not diagnose. List three questions I should consider and say what information is missing.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Missing context can change the meaning.
  • Wrong range: an adult range applied to a child gives a false picture.
  • Incorrect statements and no sources.
  • Privacy: remove names and ID numbers first.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Numbers need context, and so does AI.
  2. Use AI to explain, not to diagnose.
  3. Review before patients see it.

AI-Powered Clinical Documentation

AI drafts the note. You sign it.

1 Opening perspective

Documentation is the part of medicine most doctors would hand over first. Notes, discharge summaries and letters take hours that could go to patients. AI drafting tools are now among the most widely used clinical AI. The key question is not whether AI can write the note. It is who checks it.

2 The science behind the topic

AI can help with three kinds of documents: visit notes (from a recording or your dictation), discharge summaries (condensing days of notes) and patient-friendly summaries (plain-language rewrites). Drafting is easy to automate. Checking is not, and it cannot be skipped.

3 Clinical relevance

Compared with doctors. In a 2025 study in JAMA Internal Medicine, 22 attending physicians blindly compared AI-written and doctor-written discharge narratives for 100 admissions. Quality was comparable and the two were preferred about equally. The AI versions were less complete and had more errors, but did not differ significantly in potential harm. The authors suggest use only after human review.

In real use. In a 2026 Stanford pilot in JAMA Network Open, an AI tool drafted discharge summaries for hospitalists. Doctors used the AI text in more than half of cases. In feedback on 100 summaries, about one in four had omissions, one in five had inaccuracies and 2% had hallucinations. Time savings were small and variable, although burnout scores were lower.

For families. In a 2024 study in JAMA Network Open, GPT-4 rewrote 50 discharge summaries in plain language. They were easier to read, but physician reviewers raised safety concerns in some, mostly omissions.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Give the source text, not memory: paste de-identified notes and say “use only this text”.
  2. Ask what it left out and which statements are not in the source.
  3. Read the final version against the source, focusing on medicines, doses, dates and follow-up.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Using only the de-identified notes below, draft a hospital-course summary of no more than 150 words. Then list any important facts from the notes that you left out, and flag any statement that is not directly supported by the notes.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Omissions are the quiet error.
  • More errors than human drafts in one blinded study.
  • Edit fatigue: reviewers may skim and sign.
  • Small time gains: the main benefit may be less effort.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI drafts, you are accountable.
  2. Check for what is missing as well as what is wrong.
  3. Measure the benefit in your own setting.

Ambient AI and Voice Documentation

Listening tools need listening consent.

1 Opening perspective

Ambient AI scribes listen to a consultation, turn speech into text and draft a note for you to review. They are among the fastest-growing clinical AI tools because they target paperwork directly. They also put a microphone in the exam room, which raises questions about accuracy, consent and privacy.

2 The science behind the topic

A scribe works in three steps. It captures the audio, transcribes speech into text, then a language model summarizes it into a note. Errors can start at each step: missed speech, invented phrases (about 1% of transcriptions in one study, Chapter 12), and omissions or inaccuracies in the note (Chapter 19). The clinician still owns the final note.

3 Clinical relevance

Time and burnout. A one-year analysis from The Permanente Medical Group (NEJM Catalyst, 2025) covered thousands of physicians and about 2.6 million patient encounters. It estimated that documentation time saved was equal to roughly 1,800 workdays compared with non-users, and surveys found most patients and physicians pleased. A 2025 study found burnout fell after 30 days of use (Chapter 4). Gains are uneven: another study found about 16 minutes saved per 8 hours of patient care, with inconsistent use.

Consent and the law. In the US, HIPAA treats scribing for documentation as part of treatment, but state recording laws are separate and some states require all parties to consent. Patients have sued health systems, alleging recording without informed consent. Rules differ by country, so check your own law and hospital policy. India’s DPDP Act is covered in Chapter 41.

In pediatrics, parents, siblings and sometimes interpreters are in the room. Make sure everyone present knows that recording is happening.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask before you record: say who is listening, why, where the audio goes, and that they can say no.
  2. Document the consent in the note.
  3. Review before signing: check medicines, doses, history and plan.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Write a short, friendly script of under 60 words for a pediatrician to explain an AI note-taking tool to a parent: what it does, where the audio goes, and that they can say no with no effect on their child’s care. The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Consent gaps can lead to lawsuits.
  • Invented speech in transcription.
  • Omissions and inaccuracies in the drafted note.
  • Uneven benefit: savings vary by user and tool.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Scribes can cut paperwork and burnout, although gains are uneven.
  2. Consent comes first: tell everyone present and document it.
  3. Review every note. You remain accountable.

Plain words, every language, checked by a person.

1 Opening perspective

Patient education only works if the patient understands it. Health agencies recommend a reading level around sixth grade, yet many online materials sit far above it. AI can rewrite and translate in seconds. The risk is that meaning gets lost on the way and a family follows instructions that no longer say what you meant.

2 The science behind the topic

AI can simplify (lower the reading level), translate (into another language) and summarize (shorten). Readable is not the same as correct, and translated is not the same as safe.

3 Clinical relevance

Simplifying. In a 2025 study in the Journal of Medical Internet Research, 60 patient education materials from three major US health organizations averaged about grade 10. After AI simplification the average fell to roughly grade 6 to 8. Understandability held up. A small share of the Gemini and Claude versions (about 3%) contained inaccuracies, and none of the ChatGPT versions did. Another 2025 study found ChatGPT lowered rheumatology articles from about 15th grade to 10th grade, still above the target.

Translating. In a 2024 study in Pediatrics from Boston Children’s, 20 standard pediatric discharge instructions were translated by professionals, Google Translate and ChatGPT-4. For Spanish and Portuguese the machines were comparable to professionals. For Haitian Creole, potentially significant errors appeared in about one in three ChatGPT translations and about one in four Google Translate translations, against under one in ten for professionals.

These studies did not test Hindi or other Indian languages. Have a bilingual clinician or translator review any translated instructions before they reach a family.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. State the reading level and audience, and ask for short sentences and no jargon.
  2. Check by back-translation: translate the result back to English and compare it with the original.
  3. Use teach-back: ask a parent to explain the instructions in their own words.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Rewrite the text below for parents at a Grade 6 reading level. Use short sentences, active voice and no jargon. Keep every warning sign and every number exactly as written. After the rewrite, list anything you shortened or removed.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Meaning loss in simplification.
  • Still too hard: some outputs stayed above the target level.
  • Language gaps: quality differs sharply between languages.
  • Dose and warning-sign omissions can be dangerous.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI can make materials simpler and multilingual, but each version needs a check.
  2. Translation quality depends on the language.
  3. Verify with back-translation and teach-back.

1 Opening perspective

2 The science behind the topic

3 Clinical relevance

Kind and fluent. In a 2023 study in JAMA Internal Medicine, evaluators compared ChatGPT answers with physician answers to 195 questions from a public online forum.   3. Review drafts before sending, and test and monitor with real questions.

  • Caring tone, wrong content.
  • Over-agreement with the user.
  • Privacy: young users may share personal information.
  • Dependence on a chatbot for serious matters.

  1. Chatbots can sound better than busy humans, but tone is not truth.
  2. Design matters: disclose, escalate, review and monitor.

AI in Hospital Management

Efficiency is a win only if every patient benefits.

1 Opening perspective

Some of the most common hospital AI is invisible to patients: scheduling, no-show prediction, staffing and bed flow. It does not touch diagnosis, so it feels safe. But operational AI changes who gets an appointment and who waits. It deserves the same evidence standard as any clinical tool.

2 The science behind the topic

Operational AI usually works in three steps: it predicts a risk (such as a missed visit), triggers an action for high-risk cases (such as a reminder call) and sometimes changes how slots are used (such as overbooking). A prediction only helps if the action built on it works and is fair.

3 Clinical relevance

A positive trial. At one US health system, among thousands of primary-care appointments with a high predicted risk of a missed visit, adding model-driven phone calls to automated reminders lowered the no-show rate from 36% to 33%. The effect was largest in Black patients.

A flat result. A 2026 study built a model to predict missed MRI appointments and randomized high-risk patients to a phone call or no call. No-shows were about the same in both groups, partly because many patients did not answer.

The wider picture. A rapid systematic review found that reminders reduced no-shows, with high-certainty evidence for prediction-based text reminders, but that the effect of prediction-based overbooking was uncertain and little was reported on cost or equity. Research on machine-learning scheduling has also warned that it can create inequalities in access to care, so effects must be measured by patient group.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what decision the prediction drives and who is affected.
  2. Pilot with a comparison group and measure what matters, split by language and neighborhood.
  3. Pair prediction with a helpful action, such as a reminder or transport help, never a penalty.

“Help me design a six-week pilot to test a no-show prediction tool in a pediatric OPD. Suggest a comparison group, three outcomes to measure, how to check results by language and neighborhood, and two ways it could unintentionally disadvantage families.”

5 Risks and limitations

  • Prediction without effect: a good model did not reduce MRI no-shows.
  • Unreached patients cannot be helped.
  • Inequity unless checked.
  • Model drift: keep monitoring.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Operational AI changes who is seen and who waits. Treat it as an intervention.
  2. The action that follows the prediction is the benefit.
  3. Measure equity from the first pilot.

Wearables and Remote Patient Monitoring

More data is not the same as better care.

1 Opening perspective

Smartwatches, home pulse oximeters and other connected devices produce a constant stream of data. These digital biomarkers promise earlier warnings and care at home. But a number from a sensor helps only if it is accurate for this patient and someone acts on it.

2 The science behind the topic

A wearable has three layers: a sensor that measures a signal such as pulse or light absorption, an algorithm that turns the signal into an alert or score, and a care pathway where someone reviews the alert and acts. Each layer can fail. A wearable is a screening signal, not a diagnosis.

3 Clinical relevance

Smartwatch rhythm alerts. The Apple Heart Study (NEJM 2019) enrolled more than 400,000 people. About one in 200 received an irregular-pulse notification. Among those who wore a confirming ECG patch, about a third had atrial fibrillation, and most notifications that were checked at the same moment matched it. The FDA issued a safety communication in 2021 and in January 2025 proposed guidance asking manufacturers to test accuracy across a range of skin pigmentation. Consumer wellness products fall outside the FDA review that this guidance covers.

The studies above involved adults. Children differ in heart rate, breathing and movement, so ask whether a device was validated in your patient’s age group.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what the device was validated against and in whom: age, skin tone and condition.
  2. Treat a home reading as a prompt for a clinical check, especially if it disagrees with how the child looks.
  3. Agree who watches the data and what follows an alert before you start.

“I am considering a home pulse oximeter and a smartwatch alert for families of children with chronic respiratory disease. List ten questions to ask the manufacturer about validation in children, skin tone, false alarms, data privacy and what happens after an alert.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • False reassurance: a normal reading can hide low oxygen.
  • False alarms and anxiety.
  • Unequal accuracy across groups.
  • Outside regulation: consumer wellness products may not face FDA review.

6 Key takeaways

  1. A wearable is a signal, not a diagnosis.
  2. Check validation by age and skin tone, not just overall accuracy.
  3. Decide who acts on the alert before the device goes home.

Can AI Improve Patient Safety?

Safety tools need safe use.

1 Opening perspective

Patient safety is about preventing harm. AI can be a safeguard, such as an early-warning system, and it can also be a new source of harm, such as a chatbot that gives wrong advice. In January 2026 the safety organization ECRI ranked the misuse of AI chatbots in healthcare as the number one health technology hazard for the year.

2 The science behind the topic

Three things decide whether AI helps safety. Early-warning tools only help if there is a clear owner and a fast response. New hazards arise when a tool is used for a task it was never validated for. Monitoring after go-live, through audits and incident reporting, catches problems early. An alert saves lives only when someone acts on it in time.

3 Clinical relevance

A safety gain. The TREWS sepsis tool (Nature Medicine, 2022) monitored about 590,000 patients across five US hospitals. For patients whose alert was confirmed by a clinician within 3 hours, in-hospital deaths were lower by about 3 percentage points (a relative reduction of about 19%). Only about six in ten alerts were confirmed in that time. It was an observational study, so it shows an association, and some authors disclosed ties to the company behind the tool.

A safety warning. ECRI’s 2026 report says chatbots can give plausible but wrong answers. It recommends governance committees, clinician training and regular performance audits. Chapter 13 described the other side of early warning: a widely used sepsis tool that missed many cases and generated many alerts.

The TREWS study included adults. A tool built on adult patients cannot be assumed to work in children.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Name who receives each alert, within what time, and what they must do.
  2. Track response rates and outcomes, not just the number of alerts.
  3. Report AI-related errors and near misses through your hospital’s incident system.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft a one-page response protocol for an AI sepsis alert on a pediatric ward: who sees the alert, the time to bedside review, the escalation steps and how we will audit responses each month. Mark anything that needs clinical approval.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Association is not proof.
  • Alert fatigue from too many alerts.
  • Chatbot misuse for tasks never validated.
  • Adult-only evidence: pediatric safety must be shown separately.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI can improve safety, but only inside a process that responds to it.
  2. AI can also create new hazards. Governance, training and audits are the defense.
  3. Measure response and outcomes, not just alerts.

AI in Medical Literature Search

Ask better questions, and search more than once.

1 Opening perspective

Finding the right research is a skill, and it starts before you open any tool. Chapter 8 compared AI search tools. This chapter is about how you search: framing the question, building the query and checking what you may have missed.

2 The science behind the topic

A PICO question turns a vague topic into a searchable one: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. A Boolean query combines search terms with AND, OR and NOT so that a search is precise and repeatable. Two measures matter: precision is the share of results that are relevant, and recall is the share of all relevant studies that you found. A very precise search can still miss studies.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2023 study presented at the SIGIR conference tested ChatGPT on writing Boolean queries for systematic review searches. The queries had high precision but lower recall, and the authors suggested they might suit quick reviews where missing some studies is acceptable. A 2024 reproducibility study reported significantly lower recall, and a 2025 follow-up re-examined the question because results differed between studies. AI-written queries can help, but they depend on the model and the prompt.

Chapter 8 showed that an AI research assistant found fewer than half of the studies included in four systematic reviews, against more than nine in ten for traditional searches.

For pediatric questions, add age terms such as infant, child and adolescent, and check results by age group.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Write the PICO question first.
  2. Ask AI for synonyms, MeSH terms and a Boolean query, then run it in PubMed yourself.
  3. Test recall: check that three papers you already know appear in the results.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Turn this question into a PICO and write a PubMed Boolean query with synonyms and MeSH terms: in children aged 1 to 5 years with recurrent wheeze, do inhaled corticosteroids reduce exacerbations compared with placebo? Explain each part of the query so I can check it.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Missed studies: a fluent query can still have low recall.
  • Results that cannot be repeated: save the final query and the date.
  • Invented terms: check every heading and field tag.
  • Over-trust: a long, professional-looking query is not proof of a thorough search.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Start with a clear question.
  2. AI can draft the query, but you must test it.
  3. Save and report your search so others can repeat it.

AI can speed up screening. Humans stay responsible.

1 Opening perspective

A systematic review sits near the top of the evidence pyramid, and its quality depends on not missing eligible studies. Screening thousands of titles and abstracts is slow, so AI is tempting. The risk is simple: a study the AI drops is a study the review never sees.

2 The science behind the topic

In a review, AI can help with searching (Chapter 27), screening (labelling abstracts as include, exclude or unsure) and data extraction (pulling numbers from papers). In screening, sensitivity matters most, because a missed eligible study can bias the result. Specificity decides how much extra work is left for humans.

3 Clinical relevance

  2. Pilot first: run the AI on 100 to 200 abstracts you have already screened and count the misses.
  3. Record the tool, version, prompt and date in your methods.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Missed studies even with good average accuracy.
  • Results vary by model and setup.
  • Output can change between runs and versions.
  • Transparency: readers need to know exactly how AI was used.

  1. AI can speed up screening, but missed studies bias reviews.
  2. Validate on your own data before you rely on a tool.

AI can speed up statistics. It cannot fix a weak design.

1 Opening perspective

Many doctors learn statistics late and use it rarely, so an AI that can name the right test and write the code feels like a gift. AI tools can support parts of a statistical workflow, but they do not replace good study design, statistical expertise or honest reporting.

2 The science behind the topic

3 Clinical relevance

  1. Ask for the analysis plan and its assumptions, not just the answer.
  2. Have a qualified statistician review the plan.
  3. Reproduce the results independently.

“Here is my study design: [describe]. Which test would you use and why? List the assumptions, how to check each one, and two common misinterpretations of the result. Write the analysis in R and tell me what I should verify independently.”

  • Version changes: results can differ between model versions.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI can accelerate statistical work. It cannot turn a weak design into reliable evidence.

1 Opening perspective

Writing a manuscript is hard, and writing in a second language is harder. AI can polish grammar, suggest structure and draft paragraphs in seconds. Journals have responded with clear rules about what is acceptable. Knowing those rules protects your paper and your reputation.

2 The science behind the topic

  • Meaning drift: a smoother sentence can say something different.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI can polish your writing, but it cannot be an author.

AI and Research Integrity

Integrity is about honesty, not detection tools.

1 Opening perspective

Research integrity means reporting what you really did and found, honestly and accurately. AI makes three old problems easier: fabricated text and data, invented references and hidden use of AI. Doctors who write, review or supervise research need to know how these risks look in practice and what protects against them.

2 The science behind the topic

Fabricated content can look polished. Invented references look real. Undisclosed AI use is hard to spot. Integrity depends on honest authors and careful editors, not on a software detector. Keep a record of your data, code, prompts and every use of AI, and never let AI fill in missing values or invent controls: fabricating data is misconduct.

3 Clinical relevance

In a 2023 study in npj Digital Medicine, ChatGPT wrote 50 medical abstracts from journal titles, with completely invented data. Plagiarism checkers found no problem. An AI-output detector did well, but blinded human reviewers, who knew some abstracts were fake, correctly identified only about two in three of the generated ones and wrongly flagged about one in seven real ones as generated.

A 2023 study in Patterns found that AI-text detectors consistently misclassified writing by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, while correctly classifying native writing. Accusing an author because of a detector score can therefore be unfair.

Chapter 1 showed that many citations written by AI were made up in one study.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Keep a research log of data, code, prompts and every use of AI.
  2. Open and read every reference you cite.
  3. Use detectors only as a reason to ask questions, never as proof of misconduct.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft a transparent AI-use statement for the acknowledgements of my manuscript. I used an AI tool to improve grammar in the Introduction and Discussion. I wrote the text, analysed the data and checked all references myself. Leave blanks for the tool name, version and date.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Hard-to-spot fakes that pass expert review.
  • False accusations: detectors can wrongly flag honest authors.
  • Fabricated references and data.
  • Plagiarism checkers miss AI text.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI can produce convincing fakes that people and plagiarism tools often miss.
  2. Do not rely on detectors alone.
  3. Keep a clear record of data, code, prompts and disclosure.

An explanation is not a guarantee.

1 Opening perspective

Many AI models are called black boxes: they give an answer but not the reasons. Explainable AI tries to open the box with heatmaps, lists of important inputs or written reasons. Regulators and clinicians hope that explanations will let doctors spot faulty reasoning before it harms a patient. Does that hope hold up?

2 The science behind the topic

Common kinds of explanation are heatmaps (where a model looked in an image), feature importance (which inputs pushed a prediction) and written rationales from a language model. An explanation is itself an AI output. It needs to be tested, not assumed to be right.

3 Clinical relevance

In a 2023 randomized vignette study in JAMA with more than 450 clinicians, a standard AI model improved diagnostic accuracy for acute respiratory failure slightly, and heatmap explanations helped a little more. A deliberately biased AI model lowered clinicians’ accuracy by about 11 percentage points, and with explanations it still lowered it by about 9. Explanations did not protect clinicians from a biased model.

A 2021 paper in Lancet Digital Health argued that current explainability methods are unlikely to deliver trust, transparency or bias protection for individual patient decisions.

A heatmap that looks sensible on an adult chest X-ray may not mean the same on a child’s anatomy. Ask for pediatric validation.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask what kind of explanation the tool gives and whether it was tested with clinicians.
  2. Read the explanation critically: does it point to clinically relevant evidence?
  3. Use it as a prompt to look at the patient again, not as a verdict.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain to a clinician in plain language what a saliency heatmap on a chest X-ray does and does not tell them. Give three examples of how a heatmap could look reasonable and still be wrong, and three questions to ask a vendor.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Explanations may not expose bias.
  • Over-reliance: a convincing explanation can increase trust in a wrong answer.
  • False reassurance.
  • Vignettes are not practice.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Explainable does not mean trustworthy.
  2. Explanations did not protect clinicians from a biased model in a rigorous test.
  3. Validate the model first, then use explanations as one signal among many.

Bias in Medical AI

Fair AI needs more than high overall accuracy.

1 Opening perspective

Medical AI can perform differently across populations. Bias may enter through unrepresentative training data, flawed labels, proxy outcomes, study design and unequal access to care. A tool can look excellent on average and still serve some patients much worse than others.

2 The science behind the topic

Bias can enter in three places. Training data: some ages, sexes, ethnic groups, languages or care settings are under-represented. Labels and proxies: for example health care cost used as a stand-in for illness. Evaluation: only overall accuracy is reported, so subgroup gaps stay hidden. Fair AI needs representative data, subgroup evaluation, local validation and continuous oversight.

3 Clinical relevance

In a 2019 study in Science, Obermeyer and colleagues examined a widely used algorithm that helps decide who gets extra care. At the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker than White patients. The algorithm predicted health care costs, not illness, and less money is spent on Black patients because of unequal access to care. Correcting this would raise the share of Black patients flagged for extra help from about 18% to about 47%.

A 2021 study in Nature Medicine found that chest X-ray models consistently underdiagnosed under-served groups, including female patients, patients under 20, Black patients, Hispanic patients and those with lower-income insurance, and most of all at the intersections of these groups. A 2022 commentary cautioned that biases in the datasets themselves make this kind of analysis hard to interpret, which is another reason to test locally.

Check age groups, developmental stages and body-size ranges. Adult performance must not be assumed to apply to children.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask who was represented in the development and validation datasets.
  2. Ask for performance by subgroup: age, sex, ethnicity, language and care setting.
  3. Validate locally and monitor errors after go-live.

“Draft ten questions a hospital committee should ask a vendor about bias in an AI tool for pediatric triage, covering training data, labels and proxies, subgroup performance by age and language, local validation and monitoring after go-live.”

5 Risks and limitations

  • Proxy outcomes can carry inequality.
  • Hidden underdiagnosis behind a good average.
  • Biased data about bias.
  • Adult-to-child assumptions.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Ask what the model was trained to predict. The label can carry bias.
  2. Demand subgroup results, including age groups.
  3. Validate locally and keep monitoring.

Data Privacy in Healthcare AI

Patient data is a trust, not training material.

1 Opening perspective

AI tools may process clinical notes, laboratory reports, medical images, voice recordings and patient histories. Patient information must be protected during collection, use, storage and sharing. Every chapter in this series has repeated one rule: keep identifiable patient data out of tools that are not approved for it. This chapter explains why that rule is stricter than it looks.

2 The science behind the topic

Four safeguards work together. Data minimisation: share only what the task needs. De-identification: remove names, ID numbers and dates, but this is not a guarantee of anonymity. Approved systems: use tools approved for the data and purpose, with clear terms on retention, access and deletion. Access control and encryption: limit who can see data and protect it in storage and transit.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2019 study in Nature Communications built a model to estimate how easily people can be identified in incomplete datasets. Earlier chapters showed that free consumer chatbots are not designed for identifiable patient data (Chapter 7), that some studies sent sensitive clinical data to ChatGPT (Chapter 6) and that lawsuits have followed recording without clear consent (Chapter 20).

Children’s data deserve extra care. A rare diagnosis, an exact age in months, a school and a town can together point to one child, even with the name removed. India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act is covered in Chapter 41.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Do not enter identifiable patient information into unapproved AI tools.
  2. Check approval, retention and access: is the tool approved for this data and purpose, how long is data kept and can it be deleted?
  3. Remove identifiers and rare detail combinations, and check image metadata.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Re-identification: a handful of details can single out a person.
  • Consumer tools: free plans are not built for patient data.
  • Secondary use and retention unless the terms say otherwise.
  • Hidden identifiers in images and files.

6 Key takeaways

  1. De-identified does not mean anonymous.
  2. Use approved tools for the intended data and purpose.
  3. When in doubt, leave it out.

India’s DPDP Act and Healthcare AI

Prepare now for rules that start in 2027.

1 Opening perspective

India’s Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 is the country’s first dedicated data protection law, and its Rules were notified on 13 November 2025. Doctors, clinics and hospitals that handle digital personal data, including through AI tools, will be “data fiduciaries”: the people who decide why and how personal data is processed. This chapter is general information, not legal advice. Please check details with your institution’s privacy or legal officer.

2 The science behind the topic

The Rules come into force in phases. The Data Protection Board and basic provisions started in November 2025. Consent manager registration follows in November 2026. The main duties, including notice and consent, security safeguards, breach reporting, children’s data and individual rights, are due from 13 May 2027. The Rules are not specific to AI, but they apply to AI tools in the same way as to any system that processes personal data.

3 Clinical relevance

Children. A child is anyone under 18, and verifiable parental consent is generally required to process a child’s data. Clinical establishments, mental health establishments and healthcare professionals are exempt from some of the children’s data restrictions, but only for providing health services and only as far as needed to protect the child’s health. The exemption does not extend to marketing.

Breaches. Organizations must inform the Data Protection Board and affected people, with a detailed report to the Board within 72 hours of becoming aware.

Penalties. Fines can reach up to Rs 250 crore for failing to keep reasonable security safeguards.

Dates and details can be amended, so confirm the current position before acting.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Map your data: list every AI tool that touches personal data and who the vendor is.
  2. Update notices and consent in plain language, with a process for parental consent where needed.
  3. Write a breach plan: who reports what to whom within 72 hours.

“Draft a plain-language privacy notice of under 150 words for parents of children attending a pediatric clinic, explaining what personal data we collect, why, who can see it, how long we keep it, how to withdraw consent and who to contact. The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Assuming the exemption covers everything: it covers health services only.
  • Vendor and cross-border data may leave your control.
  • Late preparation: eighteen months is short.
  • This is not legal advice, and the law may change.

6 Key takeaways

  1. The main DPDP duties begin on 13 May 2027.
  2. Children’s data need extra care, with a narrow exemption for health services.
  3. Start now: map your data, update consent and plan for breaches.

1 Opening perspective

In June 2021 the World Health Organization published its first global report on the ethics and governance of AI for health. 2 The science behind the topic

3 Clinical relevance

Transparency means telling families what is recorded. Accountability means the clinician and hospital stay answerable. Equity means testing across speakers and languages. Responsiveness means monitoring performance after go-live.

In pediatrics, involve both child and parent in decisions about data and AI, and tell families when AI is used in their care.

Governance turns good intentions into safe practice.

1 Opening perspective

Governance sounds bureaucratic, but it is simply how a hospital decides which AI tools to use, checks that they work, and notices when they stop working. Cybersecurity for Practicing Physicians

One stolen password can take a hospital offline.

1 Opening perspective

Cybersecurity can feel like an IT problem, but when hospital systems go down, doctors are the ones writing prescriptions on paper and phoning for results. Every AI tool in this series depends on connected systems and patient data, so digital safety is part of patient safety.

2 The science behind the topic

Three threats are the most common. Phishing: a fake message tricks you into giving a password or opening a file. Stolen credentials: a leaked or reused password lets an attacker simply log in. Ransomware: malware locks systems until a payment is made. The simple defenses are checking senders, multi-factor authentication with unique passwords, and tested backups with a practiced downtime plan.

3 Clinical relevance

AIIMS Delhi, 2022. On 23 November 2022 the eHospital servers of AIIMS Delhi went down in a suspected ransomware attack. Outpatient and inpatient services, the lab, billing, report generation and appointments were disrupted, and the hospital worked in manual mode for more than a week. Reports said the main server and its first backup were corrupted. Police registered a case of extortion and cyber terrorism.

Change Healthcare, 2024. Attackers logged in to a remote-access portal at Change Healthcare using stolen credentials. The portal had no multi-factor authentication, as the UnitedHealth Group CEO told the US Congress. The breach affected about 190 million people, the largest healthcare data breach in US history, and disrupted claims and pharmacy systems across the country.

In both cases care continued only because staff could work without computers. Children’s records follow them for decades, so pediatric practices deserve the same protection.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, the health record, remote access and cloud storage.
  2. Use a password manager and a different password for every account.
  3. Know your downtime plan: where are the paper forms, who do you call, and how do you reach families? Rehearse it once a year.

  • Phishing can fool anyone.
  • Reused passwords.
  • Outside devices and unapproved apps on hospital networks.
  • Single point of failure: even the first backup was corrupted at AIIMS.

  1. Cybersecurity is patient safety.
  2. Multi-factor authentication and unique passwords stop many attacks.
  3. Practice downtime before you need it.

Human-in-the-Loop Healthcare

A human in the loop must be an engaged human.

1 Opening perspective

Almost every AI deployment promises that a clinician will review the output. This idea is called human-in-the-loop, and it is the main reason doctors remain accountable for AI-assisted care. But a human in the loop is only a safeguard if the human really engages. This chapter asks whether review works.

2 The science behind the topic

Oversight comes in three common forms: a human reviews every output before it is used, a human monitors and can step in, or the AI is fully autonomous. Oversight only works if the human has the time, skill and information to disagree with the AI.

3 Clinical relevance

In a 2024 study in Lancet Digital Health from Mass General Brigham, six radiation oncologists reviewed GPT-4 drafts of replies to 100 simulated cancer patient messages. They judged most drafts safe, and about six in ten acceptable without editing. If left unedited, about 7% of drafts could cause severe harm and under 1% could cause death, most often because the draft did not tell the patient to seek urgent care. Physicians believed about three in ten of the AI drafts had been written by a human. And the doctor-edited replies ended up closer to the AI drafts than to what the doctors wrote on their own.

The same pattern appears elsewhere: radiologists lost accuracy when an AI suggestion was wrong (Chapter 17), a biased model lowered clinicians’ accuracy even with explanations (Chapter 37), and doctors with GPT-4 did no better than doctors alone at diagnosis (Chapter 14).

WHO’s principles say people must remain answerable when AI causes harm (Chapter 42). In pediatrics, review must also check age, weight, doses and the family’s situation.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Slow down where harm is greatest: doses, urgency instructions, allergies and follow-up.
  2. Write your own answer first when stakes are high, so the AI draft does not anchor you.
  3. Track how often you change AI drafts. If it is almost never, ask whether you are really reviewing.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Automation bias: a fluent draft invites quick approval.
  • Anchoring: edits stay close to the AI’s wording.
  • Human-sounding errors.
  • Time pressure weakens review.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Doctors stay accountable. A human in the loop is a duty, not a formality.
  2. Review fails when it is rushed or anchored.
  3. Check the highest-risk details first.

Digital Health Transformation in India

Infrastructure is built. Adoption is the next chapter.

1 Opening perspective

India has built one of the world’s largest digital health platforms, the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM), launched in September 2021. For doctors it matters for two reasons. It changes daily practice through health IDs, linked records and digital registration, and it creates the structured data that future AI tools will depend on.

2 The science behind the topic

Three building blocks matter to a doctor. ABHA is a 14-digit health account number that lets patients link and share their records with consent. The facility and professional registries are verified lists of health facilities and practitioners. Consent-based record exchange moves records between providers only with patient approval, using common standards, and needs ABDM-enabled software in your clinic.

3 Clinical relevance

The Press Information Bureau reported that, as of 12 August 2026, about 96 crore ABHA numbers had been created, over 110 crore health records had been linked, and more than 5 lakh health facilities and over 10 lakh healthcare professionals were registered. Linked records passed 100 crore by May 2026.

Counts are not outcomes. These numbers show reach, not quality of care. Reviews and commentary point to four barriers: connecting the new standards to older hospital software, cost and skills gaps in small providers, doctors’ worry that digital records will slow them down, and uneven digital literacy among patients.

AI tools learn from data (Chapter 3), so the quality, consent and privacy of linked records will shape what AI can safely do (Chapters 38 to 41).

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Register yourself and your clinic in the national professional and facility registries.
  2. Ask your software vendor whether it is ABDM-enabled and how it handles consent and security.
  3. Pick one workflow, such as ABHA-linked registration, and measure the time it saves.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft a six-step plan for a small pediatric clinic to start using ABHA-linked digital records: registering the clinic and doctors, choosing ABDM-enabled software, training staff, explaining consent to parents, protecting data, and measuring what improves after three months.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Numbers versus use: accounts created are not the same as better care.
  • Older systems may be hard to connect.
  • Cost and skills in small practices.
  • Privacy: consent and the DPDP rules apply to health data (Chapter 41).

6 Key takeaways

  1. India’s digital health backbone is at national scale.
  2. Adoption quality, not account counts, will decide the benefit.
  3. Good AI will need good, consented, well-protected data.

Why Doctors Must Participate in AI Development

If clinicians are not at the table, the tool is built without them.

1 Opening perspective

Many doctors feel that AI is something done to them: a tool arrives, a policy follows, and clinical work changes. But AI is built by people making choices about which problem to solve, which data to use and how to test the result. Clinical knowledge belongs in every one of those choices, and tools built without it tend to show their gaps later.

2 The science behind the topic

Clinicians add value at four stages. Problem formulation: is this worth solving, and what decision would change? Data and labels: how and why the data were collected, and whether a label means what the model assumes. Validation: tests in real workflows, ages and settings. Deployment and monitoring: workflow design, safety reporting and follow-up.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2019 roadmap in Nature Medicine by a multidisciplinary group argued that bringing machine learning into care needs a team of engaged stakeholders and a systematic process from problem formulation to deployment. It noted that many researchers work on questions simply because labelled data exist, and that engaging clinicians early helps identify the right problem.

A 2025 study in JAMA Health Forum looked at 950 FDA-cleared AI-enabled devices. About 6% were linked to recalls. Lack of reported clinical validation was independently associated with recall, and most of these devices were cleared through a pathway that usually does not require clinical studies.

Pediatricians in particular can insist on pediatric data and validation (Chapter 15).

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Join one group: a hospital AI committee (Chapter 43), a vendor advisory panel or a research collaboration.
  2. Bring three questions: what decision changes, who was in the data, and how will harm be measured?
  3. Offer your clinic as a pilot site with a monitoring plan and a way to stop.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“I am a pediatrician invited to advise on an AI tool. Write a one-page briefing with ten questions I should ask the developers about the clinical problem, the data and labels, validation in children, workflow, safety monitoring and who is accountable.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Token involvement: being consulted late, without influence, is not participation.
  • Conflicts of interest: disclose any payments or ties.
  • Skills and time: meaningful input needs basic AI literacy.
  • Validation gaps: clearance is not proof a tool works in your setting.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Clinical expertise belongs in AI design, testing and oversight.
  2. Validation gaps are linked to recalls. Doctors can help close them.
  3. Start small: join a committee, ask three questions, pilot with monitoring.

Women in Healthcare AI Leadership

Diverse leaders build tools that serve diverse patients.

1 Opening perspective

Who leads healthcare AI shapes which problems get solved, whose data are used and who is considered when tools are tested. Women are a large part of the medical workforce, yet in research leadership the picture is uneven. This chapter looks at what authorship data show and what individual doctors can do.

2 The science behind the topic

Authorship is one rough way to look at leadership. The first author usually did the main work, often early in a career. The senior (last) author usually leads the team and steers questions and funding. Teams that reflect the patients they serve are better placed to notice who is missing from the data (Chapter 38).

3 Clinical relevance

In a 2025 analysis of nearly 800 urology AI articles from 2014 to 2024, about 18% of first authors and 12.5% of senior authors were women. In a 2024 study of 115 breast imaging AI articles, women were about 36% of first authors and 37% of senior authors, and the share of women senior authors was rising over time. In a 2025 analysis of five leading critical care journals over twenty years, only about 8% of articles had women as both first and senior author, against more than half with men in both positions.

Chapter 38 showed that chest X-ray models underdiagnosed female patients more often. Diverse teams are more likely to ask who was in the data and whether results were checked by sex.

Read the numbers with care. Authorship is only a proxy for leadership, and several studies estimated gender from names using software. Even so, a gap at the senior level appears in each of them.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Lead, not just contribute: offer to lead a pilot, a committee or a paper on AI in your own field.
  2. Sponsor, not only mentor: name and recommend a junior colleague for a visible role.
  3. Build visible AI skills and share what you learn with your department.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Draft a short, confident pitch of under 150 words asking my department head to let me lead a six-month pilot of an AI documentation tool, covering the problem, a safety plan, the evidence I will collect and the team I will include.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Authorship is a proxy for leadership.
  • Estimated gender can be wrong.
  • Tokenism: inclusion without influence changes little.
  • Data gaps in women’s health persist.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Women are under-represented at the senior level of AI-related research in the fields studied.
  2. Diverse leadership helps AI serve diverse patients.
  3. Sponsorship and visible roles move people into leadership.

Building an AI-Ready Hospital

Ready means people, process, technology and governance together.

1 Opening perspective

Being AI-ready is not about owning the newest tool. It is about having the foundations that make any tool safe and useful: people who understand it, processes that check it, technology that protects data and governance that keeps someone accountable. Earlier chapters covered each piece. This one puts them together.

2 The science behind the topic

Four pillars work together. People: AI literacy, trained users and clinical champions. Process: review steps, consent, incident reporting and clear workflows. Technology: secure, interoperable systems and approved tools. Governance: policies, validation, monitoring and accountability. A strong tool with weak governance, or strong governance with untrained users, still fails.

3 Clinical relevance

A 2025 study in Health Affairs of about 2,400 US hospitals found that about two in three used predictive models integrated with their health record. Of those, about six in ten checked the models for accuracy using their own data, and fewer than half checked for bias. Checking was more common in hospitals that built their own models, had higher operating margins or belonged to a larger system. The authors warned of a growing digital divide between hospitals.

A later US government data brief reported that use had risen to about seven in ten hospitals and that most checked accuracy and bias. Use differed sharply by type: members of multi-hospital systems used predictive AI far more often than independent hospitals.

A similar divide is likely in India between large networks and small or independent facilities (Chapter 47). Smaller hospitals can still start with AI literacy, a tool list and a few clear rules.

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Score your hospital from 0 to 3 on each of the four pillars, honestly.
  2. Fix the lowest-scoring pillar first, one gap per quarter.
  3. Share the scorecard with leadership and repeat it every year.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Create a one-page AI readiness scorecard for a 50-bed pediatric hospital covering people, process, technology and governance. For each pillar give four yes-or-no questions, a scoring guide and one first action. Keep it practical for a hospital without a data science team.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Buying before readiness.
  • Vendor models are checked locally less often.
  • A widening divide between well-resourced hospitals and others.
  • Self-reported surveys may overstate how much hospitals evaluate.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI readiness has four parts: people, process, technology and governance.
  2. Local evaluation is the weak spot in many hospitals.
  3. Start with an honest scorecard and fix one gap at a time.

The Future of Pediatric Healthcare

Predictive, preventive, personalized, and still human.

1 Opening perspective

The future of pediatric healthcare is often summed up in three words: predictive, preventive and personalized. It is an attractive picture of risk spotted early, illness prevented and care fitted to each child. This chapter asks what evidence supports each word today, and what must stay human as the tools improve.

2 The science behind the topic

Predictive means models that estimate a child’s risk before illness. Most pediatric models still lack external validation (Chapter 36). Preventive means earlier detection, such as genome screening of newborns and developmental screening aids (Chapter 21). Personalized means care and communication fitted to the child and family. Chatbots did no better than standard materials in one vaccine trial (Chapter 21). The future needs tools built and tested for children first, and clinicians who can turn probabilities into plans families understand.

3 Clinical relevance

The GUARDIAN study in New York City adds genome sequencing to standard newborn screening. Among its first 4,000 newborns, about 4% screened positive for one of more than a hundred treatable early-onset conditions, and about 3% were confirmed. Most of the confirmed cases would not have been picked up by standard screening, and most families approached agreed to take part.

Read it carefully. This was genome sequencing, not an AI tool, although AI may help interpret variants in future. The authors say more studies are needed to show whether sequencing changes management and improves outcomes. The study was funded in part by companies that sell sequencing, and not every screen-positive infant was confirmed. Equitable access to follow-up care and privacy remain open questions.

AI in pediatrics still needs pediatric-first design (Chapter 15), external validation (Chapter 36) and protection for adolescents using chatbots (Chapter 45).

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Ask of any tool described as the future: predictive of what, and what would we do differently?
  2. Ask whether there is evidence in children like yours.
  3. Plan how to explain results to parents in plain numbers and next steps.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Explain to parents, in under 120 words and plain language, what it means when a newborn genome screening result says screen positive for a treatable condition: that it is not yet a diagnosis, what confirmatory testing involves and how early treatment can help. Avoid jargon and do not alarm them.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Hype outrunning evidence: interim results are not proof of better outcomes.
  • False positives and anxiety.
  • Privacy and consent: genetic data last a lifetime.
  • Unequal access to follow-up care.

6 Key takeaways

  1. Predictive, preventive and personalized care is emerging, with varying levels of evidence.
  2. Build for children first, and validate in children.
  3. Pediatricians remain the interpreters and the child’s advocate.

The AI-Ready Doctor: A Vision for the Future

Literate, confident and accountable.

1 Opening perspective

Fifty-one chapters ago we asked why every doctor needs AI literacy. This final chapter looks back and ahead. The AI-ready doctor is not a technologist. It is a clinician who understands what AI can and cannot do, uses it with care, protects patients and their data, and helps decide how it is used.

2 The science behind the topic

Being AI-ready is a set of habits, not a piece of software. Looking back across the series: Foundations taught that fluent is not the same as factual, so verify. Clinical practice taught that AI assists and doctors decide, and children need their own evidence. Research and education taught that AI speeds up work while integrity and checking stay human. Ethics and leadership taught that we must protect data, govern tools, stay accountable and take part.

3 Clinical relevance

The American Medical Association’s 2026 survey found that about four in five US physicians now use AI in practice, up from about two in three in 2024 and fewer than four in ten in 2023. Yet most doctors said they still need more AI training, and most want to be consulted or directly involved when AI tools are adopted.

A national survey of US adults reported in JAMA Network Open found that about two in three had low trust in their health system to use AI responsibly. Closing that gap is part of the AI-ready doctor’s job: explain, disclose, protect and listen.

Seven habits. Verify (Chapters 5 and 11). Protect data (Chapters 40 and 41). Ask who was tested, including children (Chapters 3, 15 and 36). Keep oversight real (Chapter 46). Disclose and ask for consent (Chapters 20 and 45). Keep learning. Lead (Chapters 43, 48 and 49).

4 Practical AI pearl

  1. Pick one habit and apply it to one AI tool you use this month.
  2. Teach one colleague or trainee what you learned.
  3. Join or start a small AI group in your hospital, and send feedback to the vendors you use and to CLINIQ AI.

Try this prompt (general information only, never patient identifiers):

“Create a 90-day personal plan for a pediatrician to become more AI-ready: one habit per month, one measurable goal per habit, one person to teach, and one check-in question each week. Use the seven habits of verifying, protecting data, asking about children, real oversight, disclosure, learning and leadership.”

The steps above are suggested good practice that follows from the evidence in this chapter. They are not research findings.

5 Risks and limitations

  • Confidence without checking: frequent use can create false comfort.
  • Skill erosion: doctors themselves raise this concern in surveys.
  • The patient trust gap.
  • Fast change: tools and rules move faster than training.

6 Key takeaways

  1. AI readiness is built from habits: verify, protect, question, oversee, disclose, learn and lead.
  2. Doctors want training and a say, and they should have both.
  3. Trust is earned through evidence, honesty and accountability.
